Brand Transfer Gap: Why Strength in One Category Doesn't Transfer to the Next

By Sadiq Dantanko, Founder of Dala Forge. Published September 2026.

Direct answer: A business that is well-established, well-evidenced and well-cited in one category does not automatically carry that strength into a new one — and this holds true both in decades of traditional brand-extension research and in how AI systems evaluate authority today. Academic studies have documented consumer-goods brand extension failure rates between 80% and 90%. A 2026 study of 50,000 brands found AI systems treat category ownership as something to be separately earned — 89.3% of AI-search demand currently sits in categories no brand has established authority in yet, regardless of how strong that brand is elsewhere. The mechanism is old; the specific evidence is new.

Every figure below comes from a named, dated source — the same evidence standard we apply to our own client work.

How often do brand extensions into a new category actually fail?

Direct answer: More often than most businesses expect, and by a wide, consistently documented margin. Multiple academic studies place consumer-goods brand extension failure rates between 80% and 90%, including research published in the Journal of Brand Management and cited widely in extension-fit literature. Separately, research summarised by the American Marketing Association — drawing on a Journal of Marketing study — found only 30% of U.S. consumer packaged goods brand extensions survive their first two years, a success rate comparable to launching a completely new, unestablished brand from zero. Even category leaders are not exempt: documented brand-extension failures include a well-known motorcycle brand's cologne, a snack-food brand's frozen popcorn, and a fast-food chain's short-lived side product — each backed by substantial existing brand strength that did not transfer.

Does the same pattern show up in AI search visibility specifically?

Direct answer: Yes, and the data suggests it may be even more pronounced. A 2026 Semrush study analysing 50,000 brands found only 15.2% of ChatGPT-relevant categories have a clearly established owner — meaning 89.3% of AI-search demand currently sits in categories where no brand has built recognised authority, an opportunity for a business with real expertise, but also evidence that existing brand strength elsewhere does not automatically claim a new category. The same research found brand search volume — a reasonable proxy for overall brand strength — correlates with AI citation probability at only 0.334, a moderate relationship, not a strong one. A well-known brand name does not reliably translate into AI systems citing that brand for a topic it hasn't specifically built depth in.

Google's March 2026 Core Update reinforces the same pattern from the search-ranking side. The update measurably rewarded topical coherence — a domain earning consistent authority within a defined subject over time — over scattered, opportunistic publishing across unrelated topics, and 55% of websites saw ranking shifts as a result: sites with deep, focused topical authority climbed, while sites with thin or scattered coverage fell, independent of their general domain strength.

What actually determines whether authority transfers into a new category?

Direct answer: Category fit — and this is one of the more striking findings across the research, because it's the same conclusion reached independently by decades-old consumer-goods studies and by 2026 AI-search research using completely different methods. Brand-extension literature identifies category fit as the single largest determinant of extension success: the closer the new category sits to the business's demonstrated, existing expertise, the more of its accumulated trust carries over. Separately, a 2026 analysis of 400+ SEO campaigns found sites that build topical authority — deep, focused coverage within one defined subject area — see ranking gains up to 3x faster than sites relying on general domain strength alone. Both bodies of research converge on the same practical rule: a business earns authority per category, not once for the whole brand, and the size of the gap between categories determines how much of that authority actually transfers.

What does this mean for a business expanding into a new category or market?

Direct answer: Treat category entry as starting largely from zero on the evidence and authority side, even where the business itself is strong. This is exactly the moment our AI Recommendation Monitoring service is built to catch — a new product line, a new location, or a new market segment is a business-change signal that requires fresh, category-specific evidence, not an assumption that existing reputation will automatically extend to it. It's also one of the eight dimensions in our audit methodology, assessed independently from a business's overall digital footprint for exactly this reason.
